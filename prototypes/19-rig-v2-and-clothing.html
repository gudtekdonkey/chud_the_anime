One skeleton, every direction

This one is a real skeleton in three dimensions, flattened into the game's camera, so he can face any of eight directions, turn his chest against his hips and his head against his chest. Every piece of clothing is an item hung on the bones, so it follows every pose and every direction, and the loose parts are simulated cloth. Turn him, pose him and dress him below.

How the skeleton works

  • Bones: pelvis, a lower and an upper spine, neck and head; on each side a shoulder, upper arm, forearm and hand, and a hip, thigh, shin and foot. Every joint has a position in 3D: x to the right, y up, z toward the camera.
  • Three yaws: the body faces any direction. The hips can turn against that (hip yaw), the chest against the hips (twist), and the head against the chest (head yaw). "Shoulder to him, chest to us, head in profile" is just hips +50°, chest +40°, head −90°.
  • Limbs: each limb swings forward and back, folds at the knee or elbow, and spreads out sideways. The old angles carry over unchanged, so the executions' poses port directly. A hand can also reach for a point (on the hilt, say) and the elbow works itself out.
  • Projection: the camera looks down on the floor at an angle, so height stays height, and a joint nearer the camera sits a little lower on screen (0.3 px per px of depth). Every pixel carries its depth, so whatever is nearer the camera is drawn on top, frame by frame.
  • Shading: limbs on the far side of his body are the lighter plane, near ones near-black. The eyes only show when that side of the face points at the camera: two from the front, one in profile, none from behind.

How an item attaches

An item is data: its slot, the bones it hangs from, its shape measured from those bones, a depth bias for layering, a palette of blacks, and whether its loose parts are cloth. Because the shape is measured from the bones, the same item fits every pose and direction.


      
Cloth parts are verlet grids and chains: the top row is pinned to the bones, the rest falls under gravity, is pushed by the breeze and by his own motion, and is kept out of his body and legs. Stiff items (the mantle, the plates) are also pulled back toward their resting shape, so they flutter but keep their cut.
